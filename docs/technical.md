# Technical notes

How Scry works, what it copies from the game, and what to do when the game updates. Scry runs only in the game client (`BepInProcess("valheim.exe")`); a dedicated server never loads it. Nothing it shows is networked or saved, so it needs nothing from the server and no other player sees it.

## Layout of the code

`src/Model` holds everything that needs no game: kinds, search and suggestions, the list's groups, the rules for preview copies, adjustments, looks and outfits, favourites and history, links, leftovers, place names, location rules, example dungeon layouts and the startup check's fingerprints. The tests in `tests/Scry.Tests` cover that part and nothing else. `src/Catalog` reads the game into the model, `src/Preview` makes and plays the copies, `src/UI` draws the panel, and `src/Patches` holds the few Harmony patches and the startup check.

## The catalog

The catalog is read once per world, a few milliseconds each frame, starting ten seconds after the world is entered, so it is usually ready before the panel is first opened. Opened earlier, the panel says how far the reading has got, and the reading takes a bigger share of each frame while the panel waits. Nothing shows until all of it is read, since an entry's kind, links and groups are only known then.

It holds every prefab registered with the scene, every status effect in the object database, every raid the world has switched on, every location the world can place and every dungeon room it can build with, and every prefab reached through an effect list. Most sounds and many visual effects are never registered on their own: they hang off the effect lists of the items, attacks, pieces, creatures, status effects and interface that play them, so those lists are followed, including effects that point at further effects.

Leaving a world lets go of everything read of it. Each part of Scry that keeps something of a world registers how it forgets it (`WorldCaches`), and a test over Scry's own source fails when a store of world things is never emptied or its class never registers.

A prefab is the game's own when it was in the scene's lists before any mod ran, read by a first-priority prefix on `ZNetScene.Awake`, a status effect the same way on `ObjectDB.Awake`, a raid on `RandEventSystem.Awake` and `ZoneSystem.SetupLocations` (which adds the raids of the game's location lists), a location on `ZoneSystem.SetupLocations` and a room on `DungeonDB.SetupRooms`. An effect is the game's own when anything of the game plays it.

### Kinds and groups

An entry's kind comes from its components, what it does before how it looks: anything with a character is a creature, then projectiles, items and pieces. What is chopped, mined, picked or grown, or breaks into drops, is a resource. Something with only sound is a sound, particles or anything only ever played from an effect list are an effect, and the rest is other.

A mod that makes the game's own prefabs buildable (*MoreVanillaBuildPrefabs* adds a `Piece` to hundreds of them) does not change their kind. Such a mod leaves its `Piece` switched off on the prefab, which none of the game's own pieces is, so a ruin wall stays what the game made it and its facts say the mod makes it buildable. A crop planted with the cultivator is a piece, since it is built like one.

Each kind's tab groups its list by what the game itself says: items by their item type (weapons by the skill they train, armour by slot), pieces by the hammer's tabs and then by tool (a piece in several tools under the first, the hammer before the rest), creatures by faction, resources by how they are gathered, effects and sounds by the names of the effect lists that play them, projectiles by what fires them, and status effects by what gives them. Raids are named by the message the game shows when they start and listed in three groups: the raids the game rolls for (by the raid roll or a timer of their own), then the bosses' fights, then what something else starts. A boss's fight is the event its `Character.m_bossEvent` names, which the game has on while the boss's health bar shows (`RandEventSystem.GetForcedEvent`, shown while the boss is alerted within `EnemyHud.m_maxShowDistanceBoss`), for its music and weather; it has no message, so it is named for its boss, its page goes to the boss and the boss's page to it, and Enter plays its music. A raid's or a fight's *Music* plays when clicked, as a place's does, and its *Weather* says what that weather does to whoever is out in it, as `EnvSetup` sets it: wet soaks you outside without a roof (`Player.UpdateEnvStatusEffects`), cold and freezing give their statuses by day or only at night, freezing outranking cold (`EnvMan.CalculateCold`, `CalculateFreezing`), some are always dark, and each blows within a range of wind (`WeatherWords`). Within a group they come in the order the bosses are fought: a fight by its boss's health, a raid by the health of the strongest boss whose defeat it waits for, those waiting for none first (`RaidGrouping`); boss health grows from Eikthyr to the Fader, so a mod's boss falls in place too. Mods are listed by whether they add to the game, only hook into its drops or spawning, or neither, as far as Scry sees. A build tool is any item with a build table (`m_buildPieces`): the hammer, the hoe, the cultivator and any mod's own. Every tool a piece is in is noted with the tab it is on there, named as the tool names it (`ToolBook`), so a piece says what builds it and on which tab, one tool as a link to it, several as chips, and a tool lists what it builds, a row for each of its tabs in its own order. Locations are grouped by biome in the order players meet them, one placed in several biomes after those. A dungeon or camp and its rooms are one group of their own right after its biome's other places ("Black Forest · Burial Chambers"): the location first, tagged dungeon or camp with its prefab to tell its variants apart, then its rooms indented under it and tagged, entrances first, then rooms, then end caps, dividers and walls (`PlaceGrouping`, a rank within the group, `Entry.GroupRank`). A room has one home, the dungeon of its lowest theme flag (`Room.m_theme`), so a room two dungeons share (the Burial Chambers' and Hildir's crypt's) is under the first and names both. What kinds of room a dungeon is built of is in its location's bundle, so until a location building a theme is read (by selecting it, laying out its example, or reading every location) that theme's rooms wait together after every biome, under the theme's name. What is no prefab is kept under a key of its own kind (`EntryKeys`: a status effect's "se:", a raid's "raid:", a location's or room's "loc:", a mod's "mod:", each with its name), so favourites, recent and links never mistake it for a prefab of the same name. Items only creatures carry, which nothing a player meets gives, go last under *Carried by creatures*.

## Search and keys

The search matches names and takes terms (`kind:`, `has:`, `biome:`, `mod:`, `playedby:`, `station:` and `in:`), each value matched anywhere in what it names, spaces left out. Suggestions come from an index of every value each key can take, built on a worker thread once the catalog is read, each with how many entries it finds. The best one shows faint after the text; Tab takes it, and further presses cycle through the rest.

Enter takes a suggestion while one is offered for a word being typed. Otherwise it does the selection's main thing, as a double click does: a sound or effect plays, a projectile flies, a status effect shows on the person, a creature makes its first attack, a wearable item is put on or taken off, a location or room plays its music or stops it, a raid rolls another wave, and a tree, log, rock or piece is felled or broken. In the clip and effect filters Enter plays the first match. Escape leaves a text box first and closes the panel the second time; Ctrl+F goes to the search.

IMGUI's text field uses up any key that types no character, so Enter is taken before the field is drawn. It leaves Tab alone, and an unused Tab moves Unity's keyboard focus on, so Tab is taken after it. The inventory opens on Tab without checking for text boxes, so while one of the panel's boxes has the keyboard Scry releases that key press first (`ZInput.ResetButtonStatus`), as the game does with a key it has handled.

## Preview copies

Every preview is a copy of the prefab made under an inactive holder, so none of its scripts wake. While it sleeps, everything is removed except what draws, animates, lights or sounds, plus a few of the game's effect scripts that need neither a network view nor a character (light flicker, random sound clips, timed destruction, fades). Scripts go first, then joints, then colliders and bodies, respecting what each component requires. Scripts added by mods are always removed, since what they need cannot be known. Only then is the copy placed and woken. It has no network view, so nothing of it is sent or saved.

Star levels, wear and the other looks the game switches by script are read from the untouched prefab and applied to the matching parts of the copy, found by their place in the hierarchy. A prefab of a hundred and fifty parts or more, such as the person, is stripped once and kept asleep, four at most, and later copies are made from that.

## Looks and gear

Where the game switches a prefab's look by script, one kind of look is offered: a plant's growth, a fire's state, a portal open or shut, a door, a chest, a windmill turning, a smelter or fermenter at work, a crafting station in use, a saddle, a pickable picked, a humanoid's gear, or an item's style. Each is set as the game's own method sets it (`Fireplace.UpdateState`, `Door.SetState`, `Smelter.UpdateState`, `ItemStyle.Setup` and so on), and those methods are among the ones the startup check fingerprints.

Gear goes on as `VisEquipment.AttachItem` puts it on: an `attach` part hangs on its bone, and an `attach_skin` part is bound to the body's bones. Armour made for a skeleton with a different number of bones is left off rather than drawn twisted. A creature that rolls its gear (`Humanoid.GiveDefaultItems`) gets a row for each list it rolls from, and one weapon in hand at a time, as its AI holds one in a fight; the items in hand set its stance as `Humanoid.SetupAnimationState` does.

An item shown worn goes on a copy of the player the same way, over whatever is kept on, and chest and leg armour paint the body with their own textures, as the game swaps them.

## Animations

The animator's switches only move it between states under conditions the game sets together, and those cannot be read at run time, so a switch pulled on its own often does nothing. Scry instead plays each clip of the controller directly on the copy through a playable graph, and destroys the graph when the clip ends.

A clip's events are answered by Scry when every event the creature's clips send is one the game's `CharacterAnimEvent` or `AnimationEffect` knows: effects play at the bone they name, props hang on their bone, parts show and hide, and a strike plays its attack. When a clip sends anything else, as some mods' clips do, its events stay off, since an event nobody hears makes Unity log an error each time.

Footsteps are not events in the game: its walk animations set a value that changes sign as a foot comes down, which a clip played on its own does not set. So while a moving clip plays, the feet named by the prefab's `FootStep` are watched, and a step sounds where one comes down, chosen from the creature's step table for the ground picked in the panel.

### What the animator plays

Which clip a trigger or switch leads to cannot be read either. So once per creature (and for the person, once per weapon stance) each is pulled on a hidden copy and watched beside a run left alone, both seeded alike since the game picks idle clips at random (`RandomIdle`). The watch takes about four milliseconds a frame, stops when another entry is chosen, and waits while the panel is closed. A watch that fails is tried once more, then given up and told in the log.

An attack is watched as the game watches one (`Humanoid.InAttack`): every clip played while in a state tagged "attack" is the attack's, in order. The same watch finds where the game's own actions lead: jumping, eating, sleeping and waking, being alerted, dying, staggering, spawning, swimming and flying. Where the watch could not see a swim or a jump at all, clips named for them are paired by their names and listed apart under *Found by name*.

### Attacks and what clips play

The game gives creatures their attacks as items and starts each by an animator trigger, so an attack plays through its clips. As its first clip starts, its start and trail effects play where the attack comes from (`Attack.GetAttackOrigin`); when a clip says it strikes, its trigger plays, and its hit plays where it lands: a swing's where a target within its attack range would stand, an area attack's in its middle, a throw's where the thrown thing lands. An attack none of whose clips says when it strikes strikes halfway through its first.

A clip one of the game's actions leads to plays the effects the game plays with that action. Some clips the game plays nothing with, yet something is heard around them, and those are listed under *Heard around it*: an alert call after waking, the idle sound with idle clips (`BaseAI.DoIdleSound`), and a hit with a stagger.

## Effects

What an effect list's chip does is one rule (`ChipPlan`): a creature's death with a ragdoll falls as the ragdoll; a list the game plays when destroying the prefab destroys the copy; a tree's hit shakes its trunk; a jump, death, alert, waking, eating or block sets the animator as the game does; an item's attack plays in the clip the person swings it in; a list the game plays with a clip plays that clip; anything else just plays.

A destroyed copy is hidden while what it leaves falls, then stands again. What falls is what the game leaves: debris thrown as the game's `Gibber` throws it, the piece's own parts when it breaks apart (`Destructible.CreateFragments`), a tree's log tipped away from you beside its stump (`TreeBase.SpawnLog`), a log's halves (`TreeLog.Destroy`), what a rock leaves in its place (`Destructible.Destroy`), and what a drop table rolls.

Falling copies keep their bodies and colliders on Scry's own layer, which meets only the ground and itself, so in the world they land on the real ground while players, creatures, arrows and the game's checks pass through them. A body is pushed out of what it overlaps no faster than the game allows (1 m/s), so a log's halves part gently. On the stage they land on an invisible ground under the model.

What a model plays is shown at the size the model is shown, so at twice the size its hits, steps and effects are twice as big. A sound or effect lists under *Plays in* the effect lists it is part of, lists alike merged into one row. A play button stays lit until everything it started has finished, and a second click stops it.

A status effect with something to see shows on a person on the stage, its start effects placed where the game places them on a character. The status effect itself is never applied.

The *Volume* slider under *Adjust* plays the selection from silent to twice the game's loudness and goes back to the game's loudness with every new selection, so a sound turned up never carries over to one that plays as soon as it is selected. An audio source cannot go above full and the game's sound script sets its volume on every play, so Scry's copies carry a small audio filter (`OnAudioFilterRead`) that scales the samples and clips them at full scale. The game's own sounds are left alone.

## What the details tell

With the catalog, Scry reads where things live and where they come from: the running spawn systems' lists, spawn points, raids, nests, the world's vegetation, every drop table on any prefab (an item tells its share of a roll in each, how many rolls and how often, as `DropTable.GetDropList` rolls it), every station that turns one item into another, what producers make, what traders sell, and which boss each altar summons with what; an altar of item stands, such as Moder's, takes no offering but needs every stand of its prefix within its range filled (`OfferingBowl.Interact`, `FindItemStands`), so it tells the stands' item and how many stands. Items learn what they are used for: recipes, pieces, stations, fuel and what eats them. Status effects learn what gives them. Rows longer than twenty show eighteen until asked for the rest.

Where a detail follows one of the game's rules, it is worked out as the game's code does, and that code is among what the startup check fingerprints. An item's facts follow its tooltip (`ItemDrop.ItemData.GetTooltip`); creature drops follow `CharacterDrop.GenerateDropList`, which leaves the top of each range out and doubles marked drops per star; sight follows `BaseAI.CanSeeTarget`, which measures the view angle to either side of forward, so the field is twice that angle; a creature turns on what it sees nearer than its alert range, less while the target sneaks, and gives up a chase beyond its chase distance from where it spawned (`MonsterAI.UpdateAI`, `MonsterAI.UpdateTarget`); a weak spot's resistances take the place of the body's for a hit that lands there (`Character.GetDamageModifiers`); a piece's support comes from calling the game's own `WearNTear.GetMaterialProperties`, is lost per metre between pieces as `WearNTear.UpdateSupport` loses it, and rain, ash and snow wear it as `WearNTear.UpdateWear` does; a spawner checks every two seconds and spawns once its timer is past its interval, so a 10 s interval spawns every 12 s (`SpawnArea.UpdateSpawn`), picks by weight (`SpawnArea.SelectWeightedPrefab`) and rolls each further star on its own (`SpawnArea.SpawnOne`); raids are rolled every so many minutes at a chance and one is picked among those that can start (`RandEventSystem.UpdateRandomEvent`, `StartRandomEvent`), come for a player in their biomes with a base value of three within 20 m where they need a base (`GetValidEventPoints`, `CheckBase`, `EffectArea.GetBaseValue`), and bring each creature every so often at a chance while fewer than its cap are near (`SpawnSystem.UpdateSpawnList`); stars add health as `Character.SetupMaxHealth` and damage as `Attack.GetLevelDamageFactor`; upgrade kits are asked for only at the upgrade station (`CraftingStation.m_upgrader`), and comfort counts only the best piece of each group (`SE_Rested.CalculateComfortLevel`).

The game's plain damage, which no resistance lessens (`HitData.ApplyResistance`), is called "true". Numbers are the prefab's own. A world whose settings change them says so and by how much, without changing the figures.

A status effect lists what differs from a fresh one of its type, and a stats effect tells its stats as the game's tooltip does (`SE_Stats.GetTooltipString`). Which mod added a prefab comes from Jotunn's registry, read by reflection so Scry does not depend on Jotunn; otherwise it is the mod shipping the asset bundle the prefab came from.

A mod can also change what the prefabs say without touching them, by hooking into the game's own code: Jewelcrafting adds gems to a creature's drops as it dies, Epic Loot rolls tables of its own and spawns what they give. Scry reads which mods do so from Harmony's record of every patch (`Harmony.GetPatchInfo`), each patch's mod being the plugin whose assembly holds it (`ModHooks`): hooks into what a creature drops (`CharacterDrop.OnDeath`, `GenerateDropList`, `DropItems`, `Ragdoll.SpawnLoot`), into what a drop table, chest filling or pickable gives (`DropTable.GetDropList`, `GetDropListItems`, `Container.AddDefaultItems`, `DropOnDestroyed.OnDestroyed`, `Pickable.RPC_Pick`, `Pickable.Drop`), and into spawning (`SpawnSystem.IsSpawnPointGood`, `UpdateSpawnList`, `Spawn`, `SpawnArea.SpawnOne`, `CreatureSpawner.Spawn`). A creature's death (`Character.OnDeath`) is hooked for many reasons, so a hook there counts only when its own code names a drop list's type or a member of it (`IlShape.Names`, each token resolved by the hook's module). A creature then names the mods hooking into its drops and, where it spawns by itself, into its spawning, and anything whose drop table holds something names those hooking into what it gives (`ModHookWords`); the details stop short of saying what they change, which only the mods know. A method a game update renamed is told as a fault rather than leaving its mods unnamed.

What a mod adds as a creature dies shows once it is seen: Scry watches the loot of every death whose loot comes out on this machine (`DropWatch`), whichever mod put it there, and keeps it in `BepInEx/config/isimp.Scry/seen-drops.txt`, written at most every half minute and on quitting, so it grows from session to session. A death drops its loot at once (`CharacterDrop.OnDeath`, while its drops are on) or through its ragdoll as that goes (`Ragdoll.SpawnLoot`, the creature noted when `Ragdoll.Setup` is given its loot); every item that comes to be (`ItemDrop.Awake`) while either runs, the game's and any a mod spawns in its own hook there, is counted as that creature's, first and last among the hooks so a mod's are inside. Each death is a kill of its creature, and each item is counted with the kills it came in and the fewest and most at a time (`SeenDrops`). A creature shows *Seen dropping in your play* with how many kills that is from, an item names each creature seen dropping it among where it comes from, and an item seen dropping has a source in the mod report. The hooks only watch and change nothing. Loot another player's machine makes is not seen, nor loot of a ragdoll set up before the game was started; what is seen is what happened, not the odds, and a rare drop may not have come yet.

Every mod loaded (`Chainloader.PluginInfos`) is an entry of its own in a *Mods* tab, made last, once every entry knows the mod that added it. Its card tells its version and id, and its page what it adds, a row for each kind, its stations and tools with what Scry links for each, which of the rules above it hooks into, and what Scry could not place (`Facts.Mods`). An entry a mod added goes to that mod's page from the line naming it.

The mod report sums up each mod (`ModReport`, read from the catalog by `ModReportReader` when it is opened): how many of each kind it adds, its crafting stations with what is made at each, built near it and upgrades it, its build tools with how many pieces each builds, which of the rules above it hooks into, and what Scry could not place: a station nothing is made or built at, an item with no recipe, drop, maker, trader or location Scry sees, a creature no spawn list, spawn point, altar or location places, a piece in no build menu. What could not be placed may come from the mod's own code; until every location is read, what is found only in locations counts as having no source. It takes the list's place, as the search's help does, from the bar above a list showing only what mods added, or with `/scry mods`; a mod's name goes to its page, and every station, tool and thing not placed is a chip going to it. Changing the list (the search, a tab, favourites, recent or where things are from) puts the list back; going to an entry from the report leaves it open.

## Locations

Where things are found in the world's locations and dungeon rooms is read only on request: with *Read all locations*, with `/scry locations`, or by itself when `ReadLocationsAutomatically` is on. The game keeps locations and rooms as soft references to asset bundles it loads while building a zone, and loading its 550 or so at once stalls the game for seconds. So one at a time is loaded in the background, read three milliseconds a frame, and released: two to three minutes in all, about two seconds of it Scry's own work. Only names are kept. Nothing shows until all is read, so a reading can be stopped at any point and leaves the world as it was. A place in an entry's *Found in* goes to the location of that name, the first one where several share it, as the variants of a dungeon do; a dungeon room's place is its dungeon's (`Places.LocationNamed`). A place no location goes by, a mod's room no dungeon here is built with, searches for what is found there instead.

Every location and room has an entry from the start, made from what the game keeps of it in memory without loading its bundle: a location from the world's location list (`ZoneSystem.m_locations`, one entry for all the rule sets of one prefab), a room from the dungeon database. A location's details tell the rules each rule set places it by, as `ZoneSystem.GenerateLocationsTimeSliced` applies them to every point it tries, each rule left out while it is at its default. Woods are told by the forest factor (`WorldGenerator.GetForestFactor`), noise from 0 for the thickest woods to about 2.2, with the Meadows wooded below 1.15. The ground value a location asks for is the lava in the Ashlands (from 0.6 the game counts it as lava), the Mistlands' growth, and 0 in every other biome (`WorldGenerator.GetBiomeHeight`), so there a minimum is never met and a maximum always is; a check of the surroundings, which keeps spots with more of that value around them than the average spot meeting the other rules, is left out where the biome has none, as there it measures nothing.

Selecting one loads its bundle in the background and holds it while it is shown, one at a time; the stage says so while it loads, and a bundle that cannot be loaded says so there and in the details (`PlaceAssets`). Once loaded it is read (`PlaceReader`): its game name, the networked parts it places with how many and how likely, the creatures its spawn points place, where its vegvisirs point, the levels it sets for its spawn points, how far around it nothing can be built, how a dungeon or camp in it is built, and a room's box, kind and doorways. A part's chance multiplies every roll above it, as `ZoneSystem.SpawnLocation` and `DungeonGenerator.PlaceRoom` roll them: each `RandomSpawn` at its chance, each `RandomObject` picking one entry by weight (an entry without an object leaves the whole pick out), and a `RandomSpawn`'s object for when it is off the rest of the time. Its entry then takes the game's name, and *Read all locations* fills every entry the same way.

The stage copy is rolled the same way while it still sleeps, so the prefab itself is never touched (`PlaceCopy`), and *Roll again* makes a new copy with new rolls; it shows only for a place where something is left to chance. A part's own conditions (a biome, a height, lava, a dungeon's kind) are not applied, since the copy stands in no world. A dungeon's interior is left out of the copy: the game builds it some 5000 m above its entrance, and a camera framing both would frame neither. So is an `EnvZone`'s view of the outside, which the game draws only for someone inside. A location stands on its root, where the game stands it on the terrain, so what reaches below it is under the floor as it is under the ground; a room stands on its lowest doorway, the floor it is walked into on (`PlaceView.Ground`).

A room opens on the stage cut above head height (2.5 m) over its top floor, looked down into; a location keeps its roof. Its floors are found in the place itself (`FloorProbe`, `FloorFinder`): its copy is made with its colliders kept, rays are cast straight down over it every half metre (at most forty along its longer side), and the heights where they land on what faces up (about 45 degrees at the steepest) are counted in bands of a quarter metre, each with the bands either side. A band a fair share of the rays land around (1.5% of them, and six at the least) is a floor, those most landed on first, and one within 2 m of a floor already taken is part of it (a step, a slope, a dais), so a table or a crate makes none. The colliders are taken away once read. A location's ground, its root, is a floor too where none was found near it, as bare earth is the world's own; a room with nothing found keeps its doorways' heights (`PlaceView.Floors`), and a dungeon's example is read room by room as it is built, its floors renewed as rooms come in. Each floor is cut at head height, or just under the floor above where that is lower (0.6 m under it, but never lower than 1.4 m over the floor), so a low storey's ceiling goes whole and what stands on the floor stays (`PlaceView.CutHeights`). The stage's chip takes the roof off, opening the floor last opened, and puts it back; its arrows, and Page Up and Page Down with the mouse over the stage (over the list they page it), step a floor down, staying on the lowest, or up, and from the top floor put the roof back (`PlaceView.StepCut`). The ruler at the stage's right edge has a tick for each floor and a bar where the cut is: a click on a tick opens that floor, a click or drag elsewhere cuts there, over the floor below it (`PlaceView.LevelAt`), above the top floor puts the roof on, and the wheel over it steps floors. Shift with the wheel moves the cut too. The cut is the camera's near plane laid along that height (`Camera.CalculateObliqueMatrix`), so it opens any model whatever its meshes; the far plane, which the oblique projection tilts, is pushed out to keep the backdrop, and from below the cut nothing is cut (`Stage.Cut.cs`).

A place's runestones are read with it (`RuneStone`): every text each can give, the one it has or every one of its random texts, with their titles, a stone told once however often the place holds one alike (`PlaceRunes`). A stone with several texts gives one picked with its place in the world as the seed (`RuneStone.GetRandomText`), so a stone always gives the same one, which its section says. They show under *Runestone texts*, which folds like the other sections.

A place's music is read with it: a source of its own that `MusicLocation` starts on coming near, a piece of the game's music list that `MusicVolume` starts on stepping inside (at its chance, unless it fades in by distance), and the music of the weather an `EnvZone` sets inside (the environment's day music, else its night, morning or evening music). Enter, or a click on the *Music* line, plays the first that can be found (`MusicPreview`): the place's own clip from its loaded model, or a named piece's clip from `MusicMan.m_music`, once through, at your ears in the game's music mix and at your music volume. The game's music is muted meanwhile rather than stopped, so it goes on underneath and is heard again when the piece ends, when Enter stops it, or when anything else is selected or played.

A dungeon's rooms and a camp's tents are built when the game makes the zone, so under a dungeon's or camp's details an example layout stands in for them. The rooms of its kinds are loaded one at a time in the background and read, which fills their own entries too (`ExampleLayouts`), and then laid out by the rules `DungeonGenerator` follows for a new one, in Scry's own code and with dice of its own (`DungeonLayout`): a dungeon from a random entrance whose way in is at the generator, a room at a random open doorway for each try, stopping early once its required rooms are in, end caps and dividers on what is left open, and a door rolled for each doorway between rooms; a camp on a grid or scattered in a ring and walled. A room may not overlap one placed before it, tested a tenth of a metre smaller (`Boxes.Overlap` stands in for `Physics.ComputePenetration`), nor leave its zone's box, which lies round the generator as a new zone puts it (`DungeonLayout.Site`). The ground's tilt and height, which keep camp rooms off steep or low ground, play no part. Its plan is drawn in the stage's lower left corner, from above, north up, and the stage's *Plan* chip puts it away and back; *Another example*, beside the stage's other buttons, lays out a new one, and the line under them says what the example holds, or how far reading its rooms has got. The plan stands over the stage, which does not scroll, as its rooms are drawn turned (`GUIUtility.RotateAroundPivot`) and a turned drawing is not clipped to a scrolling view. A room under the mouse is lit on the plan whether the mouse is on the plan or on the stage, and a click on it goes to its entry. It is never a world's real layout, and no location ever shows where it is in a world.

The example is also built on the stage from copies of its rooms, a few milliseconds' worth a frame in the order they were placed, the entrance first (`Stage.StepExample`), each rolled like any place's copy. It stands from its generator (`DungeonExample.FromGenerator`): a camp's rooms around the location's own parts, where its generator is in it; a dungeon's rooms in place of its entrance, since the game builds them far above it, so the stage's *Inside* and *Outside* switch between the example and the entrance. Its page tells what its rooms hold, as its own prefab holds little: every part across its kinds of room, the loot those give (`Knowledge.LootOf`: an item lying there, a pickable's item, what any drop table holds, such as a chest's filling), and the creatures their spawn points place, each with in how many of its kinds of room it is (`PlaceParts.Across`); the rooms are read as its example is laid out, or with every location. Inside, it stands on its lowest floor and opens on its top floor like a room, its floors being where its rooms' doorways are (`PlaceView.ExampleFloors`). The bundles of the kinds of room it uses are held while it is shown, since a copy needs its bundle, and let go of when the location is left or the panel closes; the kinds it does not use are let go of a frame after the stage has let go of their copies. A room under the mouse is named, found by the ray from the camera through the room boxes below the cut (`DungeonExample.Pick`), and a click that is not a drag goes to its entry.

A place goes by the name the game shows on entering it (`Teleport.m_enterText`, "Burial Chambers") or discovering it (`Location.m_discoverLabel`). Otherwise an altar goes by its boss ("The Elder's altar") and a camp by its trader ("Haldor's camp"), and anything else by its prefab name in words, without variant numbers and the makers' tags, with creatures called as the game calls them ("GoblinCamp2" is a Fuling camp). Each place says its biomes from the world's location list. A dungeon room goes by the dungeons built with its kind of room (`DungeonGenerator.m_themes` against `Room.m_theme`), so a crypt's rooms and its entrance are one place. The `in:` search looks at a place's name, not its biome.

## Links and leftovers

Besides effect lists, prefabs point at one another in many ways, and each becomes a link shown at both ends under its own heading: sounds an animation clip names, footsteps, the items a humanoid may carry, any prefab or item a field names (a projectile an attack fires, a creature's saddle, a door's key), armour sets, station upgrades, ammo, and the status effects prefabs give. A prefab the scene does not register has no entry, yet many do their work through one (a staff's projectile raising a troll); what its own fields name is linked from its user instead.

What a prefab leaves behind is paired with it: a creature's ragdoll, a tree's log and stump, a log's halves, a rock's broken version and the debris of anything destroyed. A leftover is named after its owner ("Troll · ragdoll"), takes its owner's kind so it sits beside it in the list, and links to it. One shared by many owners is named for what it is and how many leave it ("Debris of 30").

## The stage and the world

The turntable is a copy far above the world on a layer the game does not use, with its own camera rendering into a texture the panel draws and its own lights. The world's sun and main camera ignore that layer, and fog and ambient light are set for the stage only while its camera renders. The camera frames what the copy draws; a creature stands on the bottom of its capsule, as the game stands it. The stage copy is taken down while the panel is closed.

On the stage, an effect and what a model plays are heard as if beside you, and a model's own built-in sounds are muted. While a copy stands in the world, sound comes from there instead, and the stage's copies have their sound scripts switched off, since the game lets only so many of one sound play at once (`AudioMan.RequestPlaySound`).

A model shown in the world stands where you were looking, facing you. Effects play there or on you. Projectiles are flown with the prefab's gravity and a chosen speed, and a ray stops them at the first ground or building, where their hit plays. A raid on the stage is one wave of it as the game rolls one (`RaidRoll`, `RaidCrowd`), as `SpawnSystem.UpdateSpawnList` does the first time a raid's spawners run with none of its creatures about: each creature is tried as often as its cap (once without one), each try at its chance; a try stops once the creatures the pass has spawned reach its cap, and a group of its size is cut only to the cap itself, so a pass can bring more than a cap and a later creature with a smaller cap may bring none. Each creature's stars are rolled up from its lowest level at the level-up chance (`SpawnSystem.Spawn`, `GetLevelUpChance`: its own chance or 10, with the world's level or enemy level-up rate). Left out is what depends on the world: the time of day, weather and world keys a creature waits for, finding ground for it, the biome's level-up multiplier and the distance from the world's centre its stars may wait for. The copies have no bodies to push each other aside as live creatures do, so each group stands in rings around a point of its own, every body (its capsule's radius) clear of the next and the group as wide as the game spreads it (`m_groupRadius`) where that is wider, and the groups' points lie in a ring far enough apart that no two groups touch (`CrowdLayout`); a line under the stage says what came, and *Roll again* rolls a new wave. A raid that brings no creature, such as a boss's fight, keeps its card.

Sounds are copies on the camera, kept while they play, so long clips such as music play to the end and can be skipped through. The game's sound script picks a clip at random a frame after the copy wakes, so to play one variant, the copy's list is narrowed to that clip first. Location music is started by the game's `MusicLocation` when you come near, so Scry starts it instead, at your music volume.

## Input

While the panel is open, `TextInput.IsVisible` answers yes. The game already stops moving, looking, attacking and opening its menu while a text box is up, and frees the cursor. The camera zoom reads the wheel regardless, so `ZInput.Internal_GetMouseScrollWheel`, through which every wheel read of the game passes, answers nothing while the panel is open. The block lasts one frame past closing, so the Escape that closed the panel does not open the game's menu.

The game asks for mouse look apart from movement (`PlayerController.TakeInput`), so holding the right mouse button outside the panel answers only that question yes and captures the cursor as in play. Walking is allowed whenever none of the panel's boxes has the keyboard, and `Player.SetControls` never receives attack, block or dodge while the panel is open. `WalkWhileOpen` and `LookWithRightMouse` turn these off.

## Files and settings

The settings are in `BepInEx/config/isimp.Scry.cfg`: the key that opens the panel (`OpenKey`), its scale, whether the search has the keyboard on opening, walking and looking while open, how long before a tip shows, how many entries *Recent* keeps, how long after entering a world the catalog is read, whether the locations are read by themselves, the model's spinning, whether a sound plays when selected, and the diagnostics: what the log tells and whether the self-test may run. Favourites and the panel's state (its place in both views, the stage's look, the list's width and which sections are folded) are kept in `BepInEx/config/isimp.Scry/`, so they go with the profile. Favourites of prefabs not in the game right now are kept.

## Game updates

Most of what Scry shows is read from the game as it runs, so new creatures, items and mods' prefabs appear without a change to Scry. Some previews and details follow the rules of the game's own methods, and a few features reach private members by name. The first time Scry reads the catalog in a world it checks all of these and writes one line to the log when all is as expected, and otherwise a line for each part that is missing (its feature is off) or changed (it may be slightly wrong).

The check covers the private members Scry reaches by name, each of its patches, the animation events the game answers, the layers falling copies land on, the effect scripts copies keep, the status effects and prefab it looks up by name, and the code of over a hundred game methods whose rules Scry follows. A method's code is compared by its shape (`IlShape`): the sequence of its steps, leaving out what they name, which the game numbers anew in every build, so only a rewritten method counts as changed. A coroutine's code is read from the state machine the compiler makes for it, the one its `IteratorStateMachine` attribute names, so two coroutines of one name are told apart. The list is `Compatibility.Watched`; a mod that rewrites one of these methods shows it as changed too.

`tools/shapes` keeps the list current. From the repository's folder, with `VALHEIM_DIR` set (or `--dll` and the path to `assembly_valheim.dll`), `dotnet run --project tools/shapes -c Release -- --check` lists only the methods that changed, with their old and new shapes, and those that are gone. `--update` writes the changed shapes into `Compatibility.cs` and leaves the missing ones to be fixed by hand. Given `Type.Method` names instead, it prints each overload's shape, for adding a method to the list.

A public member that is renamed or removed fails where it is used, so every part of Scry's work is caught on its own: each part of each prefab while reading, each step of reading, each part of an entry's details, each section of the panel and each preview. Patch bodies live in methods of their own, called inside a try, because Mono compiles a whole method before running it and a missing member would otherwise fail before its try begins. A failure that means the game changed is told once as the feature it turns off, and while anything is off an amber strip under the panel's header says what in one line (`OffWords`); its *Details* stand in the list's place with why, what to do and each part that is off, and copy them with Scry's and the game's versions for a report. The strip speaks of what is off rather than of the game, since a part of Scry failing turns a feature off the same way; its cross puts it away until more goes off. Any other failure is most likely one odd prefab, usually a mod's, costs only that prefab's part, and is counted in one summary after reading.

After an update, three things tell what needs doing: building against the new assemblies fails on every public member that changed; the check's line in the log names the private members, patches and methods that changed; and the summary after reading shows anything that now fails on many prefabs. For each changed method, read its new code against the rules Scry follows from it, then run `--update`.

## Logging

With `LogPreviews` off (the default), Scry writes only the check's line, how long reading the catalog and the locations took, and anything that failed. With it on, the log also tells what each effect list and clip played and whether each part was seen or heard, what each creature's animator was seen to do and its gear, what destroyed things leave and where fallen things came to rest, any frame in which Scry's own work took 20 ms or more (split by part, with about how much memory each part allocated and where a memory cleanup of the runtime ran), and every half minute how many cleanups ran and how much of the allocation was Scry's. `/scry dump` writes every renderer of the stage copy to the log, for finding out why part of a model does not show.

## Self-test

The self-test is a tool for finding faults and runs only with the `SelfTest` setting on (under Diagnostics, off by default). `/scry selftest` then tries Scry out in the world it is in and writes what it found to `BepInEx/Scry-selftest.log`, appended so earlier runs stay; with the file `BepInEx/Scry-selftest.run` present it starts by itself a few seconds after the catalog is read, once per world, and `/scry selftest stop` stops it. It uses the panel as a player would, through the same paths, in some seventy parts (`SelfTest.Scenarios`, `SelfTest.Coverage`, `SelfTest.Coverage2`).

The first parts check the startup check, the catalog and which locations and raids count as the game's own, and the catalog's soundness (every key once, every entry named and in a group, the tabs' counts adding up to all). They search by name, kind and biome and with each kind of search term, time the slowest search against 50 ms, keep favourites, *Recent* and the history, list every tab group by group, move with the arrow keys, filter by *Game* and *Mods*, see a tab with nothing for the search show every kind's matches, open the search's help, draw both views and search from `/scry`, and see the panel's own strip name the part running.

Then one entry of each kind goes on the stage and has its details read, and the details of creatures (weak spots, when they turn on you, how far they chase, what stars add), a piece (support and wear), items (what they are used for and where they are made), a workbench (what is made at it and built near it), a smelter, a bed, build tools and their pieces leading to each other (every tool, mods' too), the mods hooking into drops, loot and spawning with every hook looked at, the mod report written out whole and every mod station's pieces and recipes as the game names them checked against what Scry links, the report opened in the panel, the hooks watching deaths' loot in place and what was seen so far told on its creature and items, every mod loaded having a page that tells what it adds, a spawner and its creatures, and the odds items tell. The stage's lighting, backdrops and views are drawn, a bigger size makes a bigger copy, a creature stands with stars and in each of its looks, a slower animation slows its animator, a piece stands in every state of wear, the loudness follows its slider, and a new selection starts every adjustment afresh. A spread of every kind stands on the stage; selecting a different entry every frame leaves the last one shown, and after many others the stage holds no more than before (`Stage.Held`).

The previews come next: an attack, a clip played, paused, sought, looped and stopped, a sound played, stopped, paused and sought and in the variant chosen, an effect looped until stopped, an effect and a status effect on you, an item worn by the person and taken off, a tree felled whose debris goes again (`Stage.PlayedCount`), a copy standing in the world and a projectile fired, and *Clear world* taking them away. Closing the panel quiets it and opening it again brings the selection back. It follows every raid to what it brings and a raid to its creature and back by the creature's own line, sees the bosses' fights grouped apart from the raids in the order the bosses are fought and linked to their bosses both ways, stands a raid on the stage as a wave with every creature rolled there, rolls it again eight times, and stands every raid that brings creatures.

With the locations it loads one, sees the stage frame its entrance on its own ground, and rolls it again, reads a runestone location's texts and sees them drawn, switches between locations while they load and closes the panel on one, reads a dungeon room and sees it stand on its lowest doorway, cut open over its top floor, stepping down a floor at a time to the lowest and up to the roof, the chip taking the roof off again, the cut moving with Shift and the wheel, starting afresh when the room is left and come back to, a location keeping its roof until the chip takes it off, and the cut set anywhere as the ruler sets it; it sees a spread of places, and the towers and caves among them, each with floors found from the top down and cut over each, no collider left on a copy, and the ruler drawn. It lays out a dungeon and a camp, sees each page tell what its rooms hold and the dungeon's the loot in them, and sees each built on the stage room by room (the dungeon inside, opened on its top floor and stepped down floor by floor, a room found under the mouse, and its entrance outside; the camp around the location), sees its plan draw in the stage's corner (`ScryPanel.PlansDrawn`), follows a room and a vegvisir to their entries and back, and checks the placement rows.

Last it reads every location while one stands on the stage, which must keep its looks as bundles are loaded and let go of around it, lays out every dungeon and camp, sees every dungeon head its group with its rooms under it, stands a spread of locations and of rooms on the stage, reads every entry's details with every chip leading somewhere, sees the places items are found in go to their locations, plays a place's music as Enter does with the game's music muted meanwhile and given back after, plays every location's own music, sees every boss tell its altar and every trader what it sells, sees every runestone's texts and titles in words, and sees that closing the panel lets go of every bundle.

The frame figures leave out the self-test's own checks, which run inside Scry's update (reading every entry's details at once is no player's frame): its tick is timed as a part of its own, less the parts timed inside it, so Scry's work it calls, such as a copy made, still counts (`Timing.Own`, `FrameTimes.TotalWithout`); the most its own checks took in a frame is noted. Each part also fails when any part of Scry failed while it ran (`Faults.Count`) or a feature went off for a game change, and a part of a single prefab left out (`Faults.Skipped`) fails it too, except in the parts that read mods' locations and rooms as well, where it is only noted; each tells how long Scry's own work took in its frames (`Timing.Measuring`, `FrameStats`) and how long the part took, and the run ends with the whole run's figures, its slowest frames and its length. A part that runs out of time is ended with a cleanup that puts back what it changed, so the next starts as it expects.

While it runs, a strip under the panel's header says which part runs, how far the test has got (`ScenarioRunner.Done`, `Total`, `Current`, `FailedSoFar`) and how many failed so far, with a bar and a *Stop* button; the chat says at the start how many parts there are and how to stop it. When it ends, the strip gives its headline, and its *Details* stand in the list's place: every failed part with the checks that failed in it, every skipped part with why, how many passed, and what to do next, with *Copy summary* (`SelfTestWords`). The chat gives the headline too, and the log ends with the same summary, and begins with a key to its lines.

The scenarios run one after another on the game's clock (`ScenarioRunner`, tested on a made-up clock): a failed check, an exception or a scenario that never finishes fails that one only, and its cleanup always runs. Nothing is spawned; a scenario that would stand a copy in the world or fire one skips when something of the player's is already there. When it ends the explorer is put back whole (`Explorer.Keep` and `Restore`: search, filters, selection, recent entries and history), the previews it played are stopped, the plan's section is folded again if it was, and the panel closes again if it was closed. The locations it read stay read, as *Read all locations* leaves them. Adjustments on the entry selected before are back at their defaults, as on selecting any entry.

## Limits

A mod prefab whose look is built by its own scripts may preview bare. A dungeon location's own copy leaves out its interior, which the game builds with the zone, and its rooms stand on the stage only as an example laid out by Scry, and a location's pieces show whole where the game may wear them at random when it builds the zone. The stage is lit by its own lights, so materials that depend on the world's lighting can look different there. A few things stand in for what the game does with a target and are approximations: where a swing's hit lands, an attack with no strike marked in its clips, steps heard where a foot comes down, one projectile thrown for a burst, and clips paired only by their names.

## Building

The project builds with the .NET SDK against the game's assemblies. Set `VALHEIM_DIR` to the game folder if it is not a standard Steam install, then run `dotnet build`, which also copies the DLL into the local Gale profile (`-p:DeployDir=<folder>` puts it elsewhere). `dotnet test tests/Scry.Tests` runs the tests for everything in `src/Model`.
