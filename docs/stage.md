# The stage and the world

The stage the panel films: its chips and backdrops, the biome ground, what it keeps, its camera, and previews placed in the world.

Code: `src/Model/StageGround.cs`, `src/Preview/Stage.cs`, `src/UI/ScryPanel.ViewMenu.cs`, `src/Model/AfterQuiet.cs`, `src/Preview/WaterParts.cs`, `src/Preview/Stage.Ground.cs`, `src/Preview/BiomeGround.cs`, `src/Preview/StageCreatures.cs`.

See also: [previews](previews.md), [floors](locations/floors.md); all notes in [the index](index.md).

## Chips and backdrops

The stage's top right corner holds few chips: *View*, opening a box for the spin and its speed, the backdrop, the lighting, a person beside the model for size and, while floors stand, whether the rooms below the floor opened keep their lights, each row switched by a click, a speed going on to the next of 7, 14, 28 and 56 degrees a second (`ScryPanel.ViewMenu`, `StageWords.SpinSpeeds`), the spin, its speed and the lights written to the config file, and for a place shown its example *Inside* or *Outside* and its *Creatures*; the roof is put on and off over the ruler, and the plan folds on itself. The backdrop is plain, a sky, the grid ruled in metres, or the ground of the model's own biome, with or without the sky (`StageGround`): of the biomes it is in, the first players meet, Meadows for none. A new player first sees the ground under the sky, until a backdrop is picked and kept (`StageGround.FirstBackdrop`).

## The biome ground

That ground is a round piece laid under the model with the world's own terrain material, each of its points coloured as the game colours its terrain to tell the biome (`Heightmap.GetBiomeColor`), nothing painted on it (`Heightmap.m_paintMaskNothing`) but the share the world keeps in the mask's fourth part, the Ashlands' lava and the Mistlands' own, here none (`StageGround.MaskShare`), a far distance for the shader to hide it by as the game gives each piece of its terrain one (`_LodHideDistance`, without which nothing draws), and for the sea's floor water over it (`_WaterLevel`), so the terrain's shader paints it as it paints the world, with the bumps it raises from each biome's own ground; it lies five centimetres under the floor, as high as those bumps rise, under the model whatever the camera does, several times as wide as what is framed and with the sky twenty times, out toward a horizon, its edge fading into the backdrop's colour or the sky's horizon under a sheet clear in its middle (`StageGround.Across`, `StageGround.Fade`).

A place paints its paths, dirt and paving on it as the game paints its terrain under it: its terrain modifiers are read off its copy while it sleeps, as stripping takes them off, and laid on the ground's mask in their order, each blending toward its colour by one less its distance over its reach, to the tenth power, times its strength (`TerrainModifier`, `Heightmap.PaintCleared`, `StageGround.Painted`); a camp's rooms paint theirs too. Each paint is noted first in the cells of a coarse grid it may reach, so each point of the mask weighs only its cell's paints rather than every one of a camp's hundreds (`StageGround.PaintMask`). As a camp's rooms come in one by one, each changing the paints, the mask is painted again and the grass scattered again once they have stopped coming for 0.3 s (`AfterQuiet`), the mask a few milliseconds a frame with the one shown kept until the new is done (`BiomeGround.StepPaint`); another entry, biome or sky paints and scatters it at once.

A place also digs and raises the ground it stands on, as the game shapes its terrain under it, so a tar pit stands in its bowl and a Burial Chambers' entrance in its sunk stair pit rather than under a flat floor (`GroundShape`, `Heightmap.ApplyModifier`): from flat ground, each of its modifiers in the order the game sorts them, on the terrain's one-metre grid, a level setting every point within its radius, or its square, to its height (`LevelTerrain`), a smooth pulling every point within its radius toward its height by one less its distance's share of the radius to its power (`SmoothTerrain2`), the ground running straight between the grid's points. The shape is read from the modifiers' places as the ground is laid, so it follows the model's size and a camp's rooms as they come (`GroundSettle`). The ground's mesh then has rings half a metre apart over the shaped ground, each point as high as the shape has it, and the grass stands at that height and grows by the game's slope rule for each kind (`ClutterSystem.m_minTilt`, `m_maxTilt`). Whatever the backdrop, the place's liquid settles over the shaped ground and is drawn as the game draws it (`LiquidVolume`, `LiquidSettle`): the game starts it as a block over the middle of its grid and lets it flow, so it fills the lowest ground joined to where it started, up to the level its volume reaches; its surface is made as the game makes its own, a point under it at least a tenth of a metre over its ground and seen, a dry one unseen, with the liquid's own material. TarPit1's tar so lies about a metre under the flat ground. The parts the game lets settle come to rest as they would in the world (`PartRest`): one that falls drops to the ground under it, or keeps its height where it falls onto solids, as the stage has none of the world's (`StaticPhysics`), one pushed up rises out of the ground, and one that floats rides the liquid where the liquid lies over the ground (`Floating`), its body's centre of mass at the liquid's level and its offset as the game floats it (`Floating.GetFloatDepth`), its pivot as far under as the centre stands over it (`PlaceAssets.CentreOfMass`, from the body's solid colliders by volume where it is given none), so a tar pit's big deposits lie on its floor under the tar and its small tar floats sunk in it. In the world a place stands on uneven land its modifiers blend into; the stage has none, so it shows the place as if built on flat ground, and a liquid there also lies on the world's solids, which the stage leaves out.

The biome's grass and small plants grow on it out to where the ground has faded away, eighty metres at the most, thinning as the ground fades so the two fade together rather than the grass ending in a ring (`StageGround.GrassOut`, `StageGround.GrassKept`), scattered patch by patch as the game scatters them (`ClutterSystem.GenerateVegPatch`), as many of each kind as the game grows at the player's grass quality, a quarter on Low, half on Medium, none Off (`StageGround.Amount`, `ClutterSystem.m_quality`), a few milliseconds a frame, a patch at least and another only where the last one's time says it ends within the share, the grass scattered last shown until the new is done, a caption on the stage naming the ground whose vegetation is laid with a track of how far it has got, shown once a stem of it grows, so never on a ground where none does (`BiomeGround.StepScatter`, `BiomeGround.Laying`, `ScryPanel.GrassCaption`): each kind for the biome with the game's own dice for the patch, the stage's patches numbered as if out in the world, as the small numbers round its middle, multiplied as the game seeds a patch, gave a cross of patches and mirrored pairs the same dice (`StageGround.PatchSeed`), kept by its forest, noise, height, slope, sea depth, vegetation and cleared-ground rules, the ground taken at a height typical of the biome (`StageGround.Altitude`). It grows short round a piece or an item, a fifth of its height at its feet growing smoothly to whole at the edge of its footprint and a margin, at least 1.5 m out round anything lower than grass grows so the grass does not hide it, and never a bare circle of ground (`StageGround.StemScale`); between such a thing and the camera, however it is turned about it, no stem rises into the line the camera looks along at its middle from as low as the stage opens or its Front and Side views look, each kept to four fifths of that line's height where it would, its tip measured from where it stands, so wood or coins lying low stay in view, while the grass past where that line rises over it grows whole (`StageGround.Short`, `GrassShort.Stem`); never round a figure taller than that, a creature, a trader, a raid's crowd or the person wearing a status effect, which stands in the grass up to its feet as in the game however wide its arms, wings or crowd; a place, a camp among them, clears it only where its own ground is painted, as the game clears it (`StageGround.ShortGrassOut`, `StageGround.StandsAs`). The kinds the game draws many times over are drawn so for the stage's camera alone (`InstanceRenderer`, `Graphics.DrawMeshInstanced`), the rest are copies. Grass sways with the game's own wind, so while the ground shows grass and the wind is more than still the stage is filmed every frame, where it is otherwise filmed only as something on it changes and the grass would jump between films (`StageFilm.GrassMoves`, `Stage.FilmDue`). While the camera itself moves (turned, zoomed, dragged, spinning, gliding or its cut moved), a full stage whose last film took at least `LighterFilmFromMs`, a lighter one counted by what it would have taken whole, (8 ms unless set, half a frame at sixty a second; 0 every stage, 1,000 none) is filmed lighter, into a picture three quarters its size with 2 samples smoothing its edges where a still film has 4, drawn stretched to the stage's, as a big dungeon's film costs tens of milliseconds a frame and the eye follows the motion rather than the edges; once the camera holds still the next frame films it whole (`StageFilm.Lighter`, `StageFilm.AsWhole`, `Stage.WholeFilmMs`, `Stage.FilmTarget`, `Stage.FilmedInMotion`). A creature or an item films in a millisecond or two and stays sharp as it turns; what is newly shown is first filmed whole. Each film measures the stage again, so one slow film, a first one drawing what was never drawn or a hitch, softens a quick stage for a frame at the most; once lighter, a stage goes whole again only under three quarters of the line, so one costing about the line keeps one look. A fast stage stays as it is, and so does a still camera with something moving on the stage. The sea's floor has the world's water over it, its surface's mesh and material with its depth and time told as the game tells them (`WaterVolume`). The sea holds what the game holds in it (`SeaHold`, `Stage.Sea.cs`): a creature that swims and does not fly keeps its root its swim depth under the surface (`Character.UpdateSwimming`, `m_swimDepth`), and something the game keeps at the water's level, the Leviathan, its root at the surface by its float offset (`Leviathan.m_alignToWaterLevel`, `m_floatOffset`). Under them the sea is as deep as the shallowest place the world's rules put them, each rule's least ocean depth and its highest altitude, the ground's height against the water (`m_minOceanDepth`, `m_maxAltitude`, thirty metres for the Leviathan), ten metres at the least, the depth the game's water shows its deepest at, and a metre clear under their lowest point; the camera frames what shows over the surface, so it looks down onto open water and sees the rest through it. The person beside such a creature swims at its own swim depth. A creature's animator is told how it moves as the game tells it every frame (`MotionSwitches`, `Stage.Motion.cs`), first as the copy is made, before its animators first run, as one first run untold can leave a state it cannot soon leave again (a Serpent stranded, its way back to swimming open only late in a clip played at a tenth of its speed), then every frame, as playing a clip may set it back: one the game starts flying (`m_flying`) that it flies and is out of the water (`Character.UpdateFlying`), so a Drake or Moder shows in flight rather than landed, which a Drake never is, and flies as high over the ground as the game keeps it at the least (`FlyHeight`, `BaseAI.m_flyAltitudeMin`), as a place's flyers stand and a raid's come; a swimming one that it is in water (`inWater`); a creature living only in water (`MonsterAI.m_avoidLand`, the Serpents) is told so on any ground, as the game never shows it out of water but stranded. Each creature of a raid's crowd is told by its own kind, every frame; a place's creatures, held in their first pose, are told once as they are made, before they are held. A Serpent's or a Leech's tail is bent as the game bends it every frame after its animator, as the copy is stripped of the game's script that does it (`Tail.CustomLateUpdate`, `TailBend`, `Stage.Tail.cs`): joint by joint from the head back, each sinking fast out of the water and slowly in it, then set back on its parent's line at most an angle off it, kept its radius over the ground, a share of the way a frame, over the stage's own sea's surface and ground, its settings taken at the stage's size. The game bends the tail of a creature that always swims; while what is shown is told it swims, the water goes by it at its swim speed (`Character.m_swimSpeed`, `TailBend.Swum`), so its tail streams out behind near the surface rather than slowly sinking as a still body's would. As the game bends it once a frame, how far a swimming tail trails would follow how long each frame took, so on the stage, whose frames run unevenly while a page is laid or a picture taken, it is bent in steps of a sixtieth of a second of its own and shown part way between the last two (`TailBend.Steps`): a long frame takes the steps it spans, a quarter second's at the most, and the tail keeps one shape rather than stretching and springing back. While one of its attacks plays (`Previews.AttackOfClip`) it slows to the pace that attack names (`Attack.m_speedFactor`, `TailBend.Toward`): a Serpent's taunt 0.2 of its ten metres a second, its bite 0.6; after, it sets off again. The game stops a creature once what it attacks is in reach (`MonsterAI.StopMoving`), but held fully still its tail rule coils the body into loops under the water, so the stage keeps it drifting at that pace, which still lets a Serpent rear steeply. Its speed eases as the game eases a swimmer's each physics step (`Character.UpdateSwimming`, `TailBend.Eased`): halved each step as it slows, half its `m_swimAcceleration` gained each step as it speeds up, two seconds back to a Serpent's ten metres a second. Only what is shown is bent, not a raid's crowd or a place's creatures. What the sea does not hold stands on its floor in the shallows, the water half its height and four metres at the most, so the camera looking down at its middle stays over the surface, which the game's water draws only from above. Turned to look from below, the camera is kept 0.3 m over the surface at the stage's size, as the game keeps its own (`GameCamera.m_minWaterDistance`, `StageCamera.OverWater`), so the sea is never seen from under it. The camera gives the sea's water the depth of what is under it, which its shader reads to show what lies beneath and soften its edges. In a dungeon's example, the game's water, as a sunken crypt's flood, which its shader draws as a flat, lit slab looking like a floor of its own, is drawn instead as a faint see-through sheet of a muddy water's green at its own level, so the water still shows where it lies and the room's floor shows through it, dimmed with its room (`WaterParts`).

The light and sky take the colours the game's most common weather in the biome has at the lighting's time of day, Studio and Day by day, Dusk by evening, Night by night, the cave keeping its own (`EnvSetup`, `StageGround.TimeFor`), and the key light casts shadows while the ground is laid. The View box's *Ground* row picks the biome: Auto for the entry's own, or any of the game's, remembered; it lies at the place's ground, so a floor opened below that cuts it away as it cuts all else, and a dungeon's inside or a room of one keeps the plain floor (`Stage.Ground.cs`).

## What the stage keeps

The ground is an object of its own that keeps everything it makes, its piece of terrain, mask, paints, grass, water and edge, and lets go of it itself (`BiomeGround`); the stage tells it each frame whether it is wanted, of which biome and where. The creatures standing on the stage are kept the same way, which wait, which stand in which room, how high each flies and which stand above a cut (`StageCreatures`), while the stage makes each copy. The example dungeon or camp standing on the stage is kept so too, its rooms, which are dimmed, what each room's rays found and the floors they make (`StageExample`), and the cut over a floor in the model (`FloorCut`); what is left in the stage itself is its camera, lights and framing.

## The stage

The stage is a preview far above the world on a layer the game does not use, with its own camera rendering into a texture the panel draws and its own lights. Filmed fast (`StageQuality`, on the settings card), the texture is half the stage's size each way with no antialiasing, drawn stretched, the biome ground's grass is left out and the rooms below a dungeon's floor opened keep no lights (`Stage.EnsureTexture`, `StageExample.KeepDimmedLights`), for a big dungeon on a weaker machine. The panel draws the texture without blending, the panel's only draw using IMGUI's blit material, so that material is drawn with once, unseen, while the panel is shut in a world (`ScryPanel.WarmStagePicture`), and a first page's picture does not ready its shader. The main camera ignores that layer, the world's directional lights (the sun, and the flash a lightning strike makes for a moment, `Thunder.DoFlash`) are kept off it while the stage is filmed (`Light.GetLights`), and fog and ambient light are set for the stage only while its camera renders. The camera frames what the copy draws, far enough off for it to fill the picture, gliding out quickly so nothing leaves the picture and back in slowly once it settles, with the floor, grid and sky reaching past it (`StageFraming`). A creature at least three times as long as it is tall and wide, as a Serpent, opens showing its side, its head the nearer end, as from the front it shows little more than its head (`StageFraming.OpensSideOn`); a view turned by the player before its copy is measured stays as it is. Such a creature is framed across the picture's width rather than its height, so its length fills a wide stage and stays inside a narrow one, never so near that its height leaves the picture (`StageFraming.LongFramedDistance`). Particles are left out of what it frames where anything else is drawn, and so is a see-through part reaching both past twice the width of the solid ones and fifty metres past it, as the beacon a Charred fortress's bell altar sends three hundred metres into the sky, which still stands on the stage (`StageFraming.FramesSeeThrough`). The wheel's and a drag's limits and the picture's rays are the camera's moves (`StageCamera`). A creature stands on the bottom of its capsule, as the game stands it. The stage copy is taken down while the panel is closed.

Everything shown on the stage keeps its last level of detail however small it is in the picture (`DetailLevels`, `Stage.KeepEveryLevel`): the game culls a part once it fills less than its group's share of the screen's height, a tenth to a third for a cave's rocks, which a place zoomed out on soon does, so its rooms went piece by piece; a part still turns to its lesser levels as it shrinks. A big place has thousands of such groups, so they are changed a millisecond's worth a frame (`Stage.StepDetail`). The biome ground's scattered growth keeps the game's culling.

On the stage, an effect and what a model plays are heard as if beside you, and a model's own built-in sounds are muted. While a copy stands in the world, sound comes from there instead, and the stage's copies have their sound scripts switched off, since the game lets only so many of one sound play at once (`AudioMan.RequestPlaySound`).

## In the world

A model shown in the world stands where you were looking, facing you. Effects play there or on you. Projectiles are flown with the prefab's gravity and a chosen speed, and a ray stops them at the first ground or building, where their hit plays. A raid comes in no waves: for as long as it lasts, each of its creatures may come again at its own interval, one with a cap refilled up to it near you, one without one more each time (`SpawnSystem.UpdateSpawnList`), which its page tells under *Keeps coming* (`RaidWords.KeepsComing`). A raid on the stage shows the first creatures to come, as the game picks them (`RaidRoll`, `RaidCrowd`), as `SpawnSystem.UpdateSpawnList` does the first time a raid's spawners run with none of its creatures about: each creature is tried as often as its cap (once without one), each try at its chance; a try stops once the creatures the pass has spawned reach its cap, and a group of its size is cut only to the cap itself, so a pass can bring more than a cap and a later creature with a smaller cap may bring none. Each creature's stars are rolled up from its lowest level at the level-up chance (`SpawnSystem.Spawn`, `GetLevelUpChance`: its own chance or 10, with the world's level or enemy level-up rate). Left out is what depends on the world: the time of day, weather and world keys a creature waits for, finding ground for it, the biome's level-up multiplier and the distance from the world's centre its stars may wait for. The copies have no bodies to push each other aside as live creatures do, so each group stands in rings around a point of its own, every body (its capsule's radius) clear of the next and the group as wide as the game spreads it (`m_groupRadius`) where that is wider, and the groups' points lie in a ring far enough apart that no two groups touch (`CrowdLayout`); a line under the stage says what came, and *Roll again* rolls a new wave. A raid that brings no creature, such as a boss's fight, keeps its card.

Sounds are copies on the camera, kept while they play, so long clips such as music play to the end and can be skipped through. The game's sound script picks a clip at random a frame after the copy wakes, so to play one variant, the copy's list is narrowed to that clip first. Location music is started by the game's `MusicLocation` when you come near, so Scry starts it instead, at your music volume.
